# Lesson 1.1.6 — Workflows versus State Machines

**Cluster 1.1 · Chapter 2 · Type A Java evolution + Type D academy evolution.**

**Engineering question:** Why is a workflow not a single state diagram?

**Source and evidence boundary.** [Lesson 1.1.5](./1.1.5.md) established the customer journey and its independent authorities; the [business process map](../../docs/domain/CLUSTER_1_1_LESSON_1_1_5_WORKFLOW_PROCESS_MAP.md) and [POL-01–POL-10 backlog](../../docs/domain/CLUSTER_1_1_LESSON_1_1_5_DECISION_BACKLOG.md) remain analytical inputs, **not approved rules**. The Java baseline has real, process-local intake, typed OrderId/CustomerId/RestaurantId and an OrderRequestRecorded post-save local fact. The new isolated Java lifecycle models teach transitions; they do not contact merchants, banks or couriers. Every illustrative financial/merchant transition must be distinguished from verified external evidence. Nothing here introduces services, messaging, real refunds or a distributed commit.

## Orient — One dinner, several independent answers

A customer orders dinner, sees "request received," and later sees "awaiting restaurant." A hypothetical financial provider has authorized an amount. Has the order been accepted? No: the provider is authorized to describe its financial operation, not a merchant's kitchen commitment. Has the entire workflow stopped? Also no: an independent financial lifecycle may progress while merchant decision remains pending. A single status label such as `ORDER_AUTHORIZED_WAITING_FOR_MERCHANT` begins to encode unrelated life histories into one field.

A second hypothetical journey is even more revealing: a restaurant rejects while a refund is pending. "Rejected" correctly describes the merchant decision; "Refund pending" correctly describes the financial remediation. Neither makes the other false. If the database has one `OrderStatus` enum and can store only one value, which fact will it discard? A model that chooses `REFUNDED` as the order's status may erase evidence that the restaurant rejected, that capture preceded rejection, or that preparation never started. This is not primarily a coding style problem. It is loss of business authority and causal history.

The objective of this lesson is to separate three questions: (1) **What state can this business entity authoritatively occupy?** (2) **What transition is permitted from that state, under whose evidence and rules?** (3) **How does the larger customer journey depend on several such entities without pretending they change atomically?** These questions are connected but have different answers. After studying them, you should be able to draw three small lifecycles, align their states at a specific instant, and explain partial progress without inventing a global transaction.

### A timed narrative can be true while no single state diagram can describe it

At T0, customer C-1 requests a meal, and the platform owns an intake decision. At T1, the platform records Order O-1. At T2, a hypothetical provider begins attempting authorization PA-1; there is no response yet. At T3, the merchant sees a request but has not accepted. At T4, a provider-like authorization is modeled. At T5, the merchant rejects because the kitchen is closing. At T6, a refund *might* be needed if funds had actually been captured—but authorization and capture are not synonymous. At T7, customer support needs to explain which of the three independent propositions is established, which is only observed, and which is unconfirmed.

A one-dimensional diagram often tempts developers to label this journey `REQUESTED → AUTHORIZING → AUTHORIZED → REJECTED → REFUNDING`. That chain is deceptive: "authorized" describes a financial attempt; "rejected" describes a merchant's decision; "refunding" describes another financial operation; and a real refund may not even be applicable after mere authorization. The diagram crosses authority boundaries without marking them. It also suggests the states replace each other, even though a payment authorization is still historical truth after a merchant rejects.

Instead, write three lines below the timeline. The **Order** line shows the existing local intake is REQUESTED. The **RestaurantDecision** line shows PENDING → REJECTED under a hypothetical merchant decision. The **PaymentAttempt** line shows NOT_STARTED → SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN → AUTHORIZED. These lines can be read together without requiring the platform to manufacture a final global status. If actual capture or refund later occurs, their facts belong to the relevant financial operation, not to the merchant's decision.

When drawing any new state diagram, explicitly state its **unit of identity, authority boundary, legal input vocabulary, state meanings and effect scope**. Without those labels a graph may be attractive while its claims are ambiguous.

## Understand — A state machine is a precise transition system

A finite state machine can be described by a finite set of states S, a set of permitted inputs or actions A, and a transition function. For a deterministic machine, `δ: S × A → Decision`, where a decision contains the next state or a reason the action is rejected. Business machines normally require more context than state and action: actor authority, identifiers, evidence, policy and concurrent versions. We can extend the conceptual decision to `δ(S, Action, Context) → Accepted(NextState, Fact) | Rejected(Reason, UnchangedState)`. This is a **specification**, not automatically a persistence transaction.

**State** is an abstraction of what matters now for future permitted behavior. It is not the entire historical record. **Transition** means moving from one relevant state to another after specified conditions have been satisfied. **Command** expresses a request to attempt a transition; it does not establish that a transition occurred. **Decision** is the result of applying rules to the request and observed facts. **Fact** is an assertion that an authoritative transition or external effect actually occurred within a stated scope. **Event message** transports that fact or a claim about it; losing the message does not necessarily reverse the effect. **Guard** is the predicate that must hold at the correct decision boundary. **Invariant** is a property that must hold for all permitted states and transitions. **Effect** may be internal state or irretrievable external work.

A pure transition function is valuable because it can be evaluated deterministically and tested against a table. However, a test proving that `AUTHORIZED + CONFIRM_CAPTURED → CAPTURED` is allowed in a teaching model does **not** prove that a bank captured money. The input "CONFIRM_CAPTURED" is an **assumption of authoritative evidence supplied to the model**. Production integration requires authenticating and correlating that evidence to the right provider operation, amount, currency and business intention. Code can model authority; it cannot manufacture authority.

The right invariant scope is crucial. "Every OrderId remains stable through an Order transition" is local and testable. "No customer can ever be charged for a rejected meal" spans merchant, financial and policy authorities and cannot be established merely by one local enum transition. State machine design begins by naming the boundary of the claim.

## Understand — Business workflow, lifecycle and orchestration are different objects

A *workflow* describes how responsibilities compose into a desired business outcome: intake, restaurant commitment, payment, preparation, handoff, delivery, and recovery paths. It is cross-actor and obligation-oriented. A *lifecycle* describes valid state evolution of a particular entity or decision: order intake, one merchant decision, or one financial attempt. *Orchestration* is an implementation technique that may coordinate observations and requests. No particular orchestration engine, bus or service topology is implied by the existence of a workflow.

Take the simple successful story: the platform records an Order request; the restaurant accepts; a provider authorizes; a kitchen prepares; a courier delivers. A workflow can place those milestones in a business relationship, yet the Order lifecycle may remain REQUESTED for the part of the course that currently implements only intake. The RestaurantDecision might move PENDING → ACCEPTED, and the PaymentAttempt might move NOT_STARTED → SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN → AUTHORIZED. These are not contradictory because they refer to different entities and authorities. A conceptual workflow phase such as "merchant accepted, payment pending" is a **derived cross-lifecycle observation**, not necessarily a new first-class state of Order.

The workflow has several clocks and evidence boundaries. It is possible that a merchant accepted at its own source but the platform has not yet received the response. The true merchant decision and the platform's observed projection differ. Likewise, a provider operation may have succeeded while its response was lost; a local PaymentAttempt may need to remain SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN pending reconciliation. A lifecycle diagram alone cannot solve that epistemic gap. It must distinguish entity truth from observation.

This separation avoids a tempting but unsound transformation: converting every row of a workflow diagram into one `OrderStatus` enum member. A workflow milestone may be a conjunction or predicate over independent states, not a mutation of one owner. Until policy POL-01 is approved, even the order of merchant acceptance and financial authorization is undecided. The lifecycle decomposition can remain valid while workflow ordering changes.

### A simple formal model, with its omitted assumptions made explicit

Let an entity machine be the tuple `M = (I, S, A, G, δ, F)`. Here `I` is an entity's stable identity; `S` is the finite state set; `A` is the requested action vocabulary; `G` is the guard/evidence context; `δ` is a total decision function; and `F` is the vocabulary of post-transition fact labels. “Total” means the function returns a defined accepted or rejected decision for **every** state/action pair, including forbidden inputs. For example, `δ(DECLINED, CONFIRM_CAPTURED)` must not silently disappear or return a null state. It should return a rejection whose next state remains DECLINED and whose fact is absent.

For a specific valid input, the abstract decision may be written:

`δ((PA-2, AUTHORIZED), CONFIRM_CAPTURED, trustedEvidence) = Accepted((PA-2, CAPTURED), CaptureFact(PA-2))`.

The *trustedEvidence* parameter is the essential qualification. Today's Java pure function accepts a named action that **stands in for** that evidence. It does not authenticate a message or call a provider. Therefore its returned label is named `IllustrativePaymentAttemptCAPTURED`, not a real `PaymentCaptured` domain event. In a future real integration, the caller must prove that the external operation and intended attempt align; an arbitrary controller cannot simply assert trusted financial authority.

A rejection is equally meaningful: `δ((PA-2, NOT_STARTED), CONFIRM_REFUNDED) = Rejected(illegalTransition, (PA-2, NOT_STARTED), noFact)`. Identity continuity and unchanged state are observable properties. If a developer mistakenly allocates a new PaymentAttemptId while transitioning, a test must flag it; it would transform state change into an unrelated operation.

What is missing from this abstraction? Persistence, concurrency control, request authentication, provider correlation, temporal ordering, financial settlement semantics and external side effects. Treat these as distinct requirements; an elegant pure function does not provide them for free.

## Understand — Identity makes state transitions belong to the same entity

In Lesson 1.1.4, the actual Java OrderId identifies an immutable intake snapshot. A customer reference and merchant identifier coexist but are not substitutes for that identity. In a lifecycle transition, state may change while identity must remain stable: `OrderId(O-7), NEW → OrderId(O-7), REQUESTED`. A transition that accidentally produces OrderId(O-8) is not a transition of the same entity. It is creation of a different entity or a correlation error.

For a merchant decision, use the pair `(OrderId, RestaurantId)` to define the locally modeled decision being inspected. The real authority would be the restaurant, not the platform, even though a pure Java class can encode the decision possibilities. A merchant's display name or address can change without changing the decision identity; an inbound callback for another merchant must not be joined because the human-readable name is similar.

For a financial attempt, use `PaymentAttemptId` separate from `OrderId`. One order may involve multiple attempts over time. For example, PA-1 may be declined and PA-2 may be authorized. If the aggregate stores only `order.paymentStatus=AUTHORIZED`, it loses which attempt the result referred to. If a delayed PA-1 decline arrives after PA-2 succeeds, a naive last-write-wins update could incorrectly mark the whole order declined. The strongly typed attempt identifier is a design prerequisite for future correlation, not proof of a real provider integration.

A composite inspection must reject mismatches: Order(O-7), RestaurantDecision(O-8, M-42), and PaymentAttempt(O-7, PA-2) should not be composed into one coherent customer journey. The Java `LifecycleSnapshot` checks that all three refer to the same OrderId, but it does not claim transactional isolation across updates. This distinction is useful: referential consistency can be enforced in a pure model while distributed consistency remains unimplemented.

### Why an Order whose state is REQUESTED can still be part of an advanced workflow

Imagine an intake O-17 whose merchant decision is hypothetically ACCEPTED while the financial attempt is SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN. Someone might insist that Order must switch to AWAITING_PAYMENT. But that change would be a domain decision: what does AWAITING_PAYMENT mean if payment already succeeded at the provider but the acknowledgement was lost? Does the Order itself have the power to establish that state? Which business policy makes it legal to advance? If the answer depends on external evidence, the platform may project a useful customer-facing milestone without making it an authoritative Order state.

The current Java class deliberately exposes `intakeLifecycle()` as a **view** of its already-recorded intake. The pure OrderLifecycle model has an instructional NEW state, but no existing `Order` record can truthfully be NEW because constructing the persisted snapshot occurs only during the intake flow. This distinction reconciles the model's pre-intake transition with the implementation's post-save fact. The `OrderRequestRecorded` object is created after save and reflects the actual local boundary; the pure transition label does not replace it.

The important invariant is not "Order is always REQUESTED." That is merely the current implementation's narrow scope. The enduring invariant is that a transition cannot silently change OrderId, that a rejected transition cannot create a successful fact, and that a payment provider's result must not be attributed to an Order-owned local method without evidence. The lifecycle can expand in later lessons while preserving those principles.

## Understand — Model the platform-owned Order intake lifecycle first

What does the current platform actually own? An HTTP command enters the PlaceOrder use case. The application validates it, applies local business rules, constructs an immutable Order snapshot, saves it to a process-local repository, and returns a post-save OrderRequestRecorded fact within that limited boundary. The public response says `intakeStatus=REQUESTED_AWAITING_RESTAURANT`, `restaurantDecision=PENDING`, and `paymentStatus=NOT_STARTED`. These fields do not establish any external decision.

The new `OrderLifecycle` models a narrow transition: `NEW + RECORD_INTAKE → REQUESTED`. It is pure: its output is a decision and an illustrative transition label, not a repository write. When read from an already-recorded `Order`, `intakeLifecycle()` provides a typed REQUESTED view preserving the original OrderId. The actual source of the intake fact remains the existing save-and-return flow, not an extra lifecycle method call. Replaying RECORD_INTAKE on REQUESTED is rejected; no second fact is emitted.

A proposed `CLOSE_UNFULFILLABLE` action is deliberately rejected even when the current modeled state is REQUESTED. Why? Actual closure may depend on a legitimate merchant rejection, payment outcome, customer notification and policy POL-02. Implementing a happy-path closure by assumption would silently approve business rules that the domain workshop has not settled. A small truthful machine can be more valuable than a comprehensive invented one.

The Order lifecycle is therefore intentionally **not** the full "order processing" lifecycle. At this point its states answer an intake question. Later lessons may add authenticated merchant decisions, preparation and fulfillment under explicit guards and authoritatively established policies. It would be misleading to call today's REQUESTED state "accepted by restaurant." Likewise, the application's API remains unchanged because this lesson needs domain modeling rather than new public commands.

### The danger of a valid transition exercised by the wrong actor

Suppose the platform's internal simulator applies ACCEPT to a PENDING RestaurantDecision and obtains ACCEPTED. The function may be locally valid and perfectly tested. But what if a customer triggered it through an unprotected endpoint? The business decision would be unauthorized even though the state transition table allowed it. A real transition guard must include *actor authorization and source provenance*, not merely a matching prior state. In this lesson, no endpoint exposes these methods and the generated fact names signal illustration.

A second risk is changing the object of the decision. A restaurant may accept basket revision B1 but the platform later display B2 with different items. If the decision record retains only OrderId and RestaurantId, future real approval must also correlate the exact commercially relevant version or immutable offered terms. This is a future contract requirement, not evidence that today's simplified local model safely handles basket amendments.

Consider temporal ambiguity: ACCEPT at T1 under B1, then a merchant operational reversal at T2. The pure model intentionally refuses a second REJECT because its local decision is terminal. That makes the boundary visible, not universally correct. A business might instead require a separate cancellation/revocation workflow, with its own authority, reason and liability. Adding an arbitrary backward edge because "users might change their mind" would conceal the commercial impact. The policy authority, not the Java enum author, must decide how revised merchant commitments work.

## Understand — Model the independent RestaurantDecision lifecycle

Consider a specific restaurant deciding whether to fulfill a specific accepted intake request. A locally modeled decision begins PENDING. Under an explicitly simplified teaching assumption, it can transition `PENDING + ACCEPT → ACCEPTED` or `PENDING + REJECT → REJECTED`. Both are terminal in this isolated model. From ACCEPTED, another ACCEPT or REJECT is rejected; from REJECTED, another action is likewise rejected. The result returns the same immutable original object and no illustrative fact.

Why terminal? Not because business policy has universally forbidden restaurant revisions. The model demonstrates what a one-shot decision machine looks like under a stated assumption. Real businesses may allow revised acceptance, cancellation, or replacement decisions; such behavior must be specified and verified later. An isolated Java type does not grant the platform permission to accept an order on behalf of a restaurant.

A proper decision needs the merchant's identity and the basket/price contract it considered. Without those, "merchant accepted" may be evidence about an old basket or another customer order. A valid proof would be attributable to the authorized merchant; a received message may be untrusted until authenticated and correlated. The `RestaurantDecisionLifecycle` class contains no adapter, message handler or remote call. Its output `IllustrativeRestaurantAccepted` or `IllustrativeRestaurantRejected` is explicitly hypothetical.

Consider a conflicting local test: start at PENDING, apply ACCEPT, then apply REJECT. The first decision yields ACCEPTED; the second returns rejection with unchanged ACCEPTED state and no new fact. This proves the deterministic isolated rule. It does **not** prove a distributed acceptance/cancellation race is solved, because no concurrent authoritative merchant state has been read or written. Later lessons will address guards, version checks and authority integration.

### The full local transition table and why it is not a provider specification

The following table is the exhaustive **allowed subset** of the 7 × 6 state/action combinations in the Java teaching model. Every omitted pair is rejected with unchanged state and no illustrative fact. The total decision surface is 42 combinations: 7 allowed and 35 rejected.

| Current state | Input action | Next state | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| NOT_STARTED | SUBMIT | SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN | An operation was *requested* in the model; result unknown |
| SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN | CONFIRM_AUTHORIZED | AUTHORIZED | Assume correlated authorization evidence |
| SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN | CONFIRM_DECLINED | DECLINED | Assume correlated decline evidence |
| SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN | CONFIRM_CAPTURED | CAPTURED | Assume correlated capture evidence |
| AUTHORIZED | CONFIRM_CAPTURED | CAPTURED | Authorization does not itself imply capture |
| CAPTURED | REQUEST_REFUND | REFUND_PENDING | Illustrative intent only; eligibility and provider submission not implemented |
| REFUND_PENDING | CONFIRM_REFUNDED | REFUNDED | Assume correlated refund-completion evidence |

**Why allow direct SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN → CAPTURED?** Some payment flows may produce a captured result without the platform separately recording an authorization milestone. This is an *illustrative abstraction*, not a claim about a particular provider's API or a universal financial sequence. Likewise, the table omits partial capture, expiry, void, multiple refunds and chargeback because those are not yet authorized requirements. It must not be used as a production provider adapter specification.

**Why can REQUEST_REFUND produce REFUND_PENDING even though POL-05 is unresolved?** Only because it is a pure educational rule showing the distinction between a refund request and completion. It makes no eligibility decision for a real customer. If reused in a production path without policy checks, it would be dangerous. The code documents that boundary; the academy must surface it whenever the edge is selected.

**Why reject all transitions from REFUNDED and DECLINED?** Those are terminal under this simplified single-attempt model. A production payment ecosystem may allow more than one refund operation, partial adjustment or later dispute; that would require separate identities, state and policy rather than blindly reopening this enum.

## Understand — Model one PaymentAttempt, not "the payment status of the order"

The payment model begins NOT_STARTED. The pure SUBMIT action yields SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN. The name is intentional. Submission alone cannot establish that a provider processed money, and the model does not possess an external provider. Under a hypothetical trusted-evidence input, SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN may move to AUTHORIZED, DECLINED or CAPTURED. A successful authorization could later become CAPTURED. A captured operation can receive a REQUEST_REFUND action and become REFUND_PENDING, and an illustrative confirmation may lead to REFUNDED.

The model explicitly rejects inappropriate shortcuts: NOT_STARTED cannot jump straight to REFUNDED; DECLINED cannot become CAPTURED through an unrelated command; CAPTURED cannot become AUTHORIZED again; REFUND_PENDING cannot become REFUNDED because someone merely clicked a refund button. These transitions are protected by the pure function's state/action table. Yet the model intentionally does not prescribe full provider semantics such as partial captures, multiple refunds, voids, chargebacks, authorization expiry or settlement. Those are future provider-contract and policy questions.

The distinction between **authorized**, **captured**, **settled**, **refund requested**, **refund processing**, and **refunded** is substantial. Authorization may reserve a potential amount without capturing; capture records a financial effect according to provider rules; settlement can be a later ledger event. A refund request is an intent; provider acceptance-for-processing is a different acknowledgement; a confirmed refund effect is still not identical to the date a cardholder sees funds posted. The teaching model compresses some of these boundaries, and its named limitations must remain visible.

The state SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN also teaches epistemic honesty. A network timeout after submission is not a definite decline. If the provider processed the operation but the response was lost, a retry might duplicate an effect without a defined idempotency contract. Future integration would need stable attempt/provider identifiers, correlated evidence and reconciliation logic. None is built in Lesson 1.1.6.

## Explore — Side-by-side state machines and transition provenance

Inspect the three lifecycle diagrams beside this topic. The Order lane has only a local NEW → REQUESTED transition plus an unresolved closure decision. The RestaurantDecision lane branches from PENDING to either ACCEPTED or REJECTED under a local teaching assumption. The PaymentAttempt lane separates NOT_STARTED, SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN, AUTHORIZED, DECLINED, CAPTURED, REFUND_PENDING and REFUNDED. Their diagrams are separate because they answer different authoritative questions.

Select any edge. The inspector should reveal its **source state, requested action, necessary evidence, responsible authority, next state, fact label, and whether the Java code actually performs an external effect**. For Order intake, source evidence is the local saved Order. For illustrative merchant and provider transitions, the source is a declared teaching assumption. No label may blur those categories. Selecting a declined payment branch should not move the RestaurantDecision or rewrite the OrderId. Selecting a merchant rejection should not magically refund a captured amount.

An important thought experiment: can a conceptual merchant reject while a financial attempt remains REFUND_PENDING? Yes. In that case the workflow's progress is partial and its financial obligation unresolved. A diagram that automatically pushes both entities to a global terminal REJECTED state has discarded an actual independent fact. Use the diagrams to locate this loss before seeing the synchronized scenario overlay.

### A worked composition with precisely stated snapshots

Assume illustrative immutable snapshots at an analyst-chosen observation cut:

| Entity identity | Local modeled state | What can be concluded | What cannot |
|---|---|---|---|
| OrderId O-41 | REQUESTED | Platform's intake exists in its current local scope | Restaurant accepted or provider charged |
| RestaurantDecision(O-41, M-42) | PENDING | The teaching model has not observed a merchant decision | Merchant certainly has not decided |
| PaymentAttempt(PA-9, O-41) | AUTHORIZED | Under model assumptions an authorization outcome was supplied | Capture, merchant acceptance or settlement |

The derived label "authorization with merchant pending" is a predicate over these three states. A later merchant REJECTED observation can change that predicate without changing the payment attempt's historical AUTHORIZED fact. If a separate captured effect existed, an additional financial remediation process might become necessary; a mere authorization may instead require release/expiry. The exact result remains a policy and provider question.

Now assume two snapshots were read independently at different times: the merchant read was at 10:00:00, the payment read at 10:00:03. The resulting tuple may be a useful **observed picture**, but it is not an atomic cross-participant snapshot. An application can honestly say "As of our latest observations, payment was authorized and restaurant acceptance was not yet known," but must not claim a global consensus decision. No central `LifecycleSnapshot` constructor can turn that observation into a distributed transaction.

In the actual Java teaching model, the constructor only requires the same OrderId in all three entries. This prevents accidental cross-order composition. It does *not* enforce business predicates such as "payment must be captured before the merchant accepts" because that rule is unresolved POL-01. Referential constraints and workflow policy constraints are separate.

## Understand — Cross-lifecycle predicates are observations, not shared authority

Consider a pure predicate: `merchant.state == PENDING && payment.state == AUTHORIZED`. It identifies "payment authorized while merchant pending" under its input assumptions. Another predicate is `merchant.state == REJECTED && payment.state == REFUND_PENDING`. It identifies a reconciliation situation. Neither predicate authorizes a transition, issues a refund, or establishes atomicity. Both simply classify a consistent set of domain-model snapshots.

Suppose the first predicate is true. The workflow may have progressed financially but not operationally. Whether the platform should ask the kitchen to start is a **business policy decision**, not implied by the predicate. It might require capture, merchant acceptance, or a specific ordering contract under POL-01. Encoding that guard now without approval would make a locally deterministic model commercially wrong.

A predicate can also be stale. Imagine merchant ACCEPTED at source while the platform still observes PENDING. The platform's state projection can truthfully describe its own known observations but not all reality. Derived workflow phases must therefore state their input evidence and freshness. A conjunction of three database fields is not a distributed consistency guarantee unless the data ownership, observation ordering and read isolation justify it.

`LifecycleSnapshot` checks only one invariant: all three entries share the same OrderId. It can demonstrate two scenarios, but it cannot prove the three states were read atomically or represent a coherent external-time snapshot. This kind of limitation belongs in code documentation and the UI, because otherwise a compelling dashboard can create unjustified confidence.

## Understand — State explosion: the price of collapsing independent lives

Suppose Order has two states, RestaurantDecision three, and PaymentAttempt seven. The naive Cartesian product contains `2 × 3 × 7 = 42` possible combined tuples even before kitchen, refund adjustments, courier custody, order amendments and disputes. Many combinations may be unreachable under a particular policy, but some apparently unusual ones—such as merchant REJECTED and payment REFUND_PENDING—are **essential** for representing failure recovery. A giant Order enum can either omit them, creating information loss, or enumerate an unwieldy catalog of mixed responsibilities.

Adding a four-state kitchen lifecycle multiplies the potential combinations to 168; a five-state delivery lifecycle makes 840. This simple calculation is an illustration of multiplicative state-space growth, not a count of reachable business paths. Counting states is not itself a sufficient reason to split services or packages. It is evidence that independent dimensions should retain their own names and authorities.

An enum like `ORDER_PAID_MERCHANT_REJECTED_REFUND_PENDING_KITCHEN_NOT_STARTED` encodes a product tuple in one overloaded symbol. Every new lifecycle option forces edits to the global enum and all consumers. A method like `order.refund()` further confuses the platform's local state with provider authority. The alternative is to model independent states, preserve identifiers, and derive an order-view summary from correlated facts when business rules justify it.

Avoid oversimplifying the other direction: three enums alone do not solve invalid combinations, concurrent writes or missed messages. Invariants across models may still need enforcement, and cross-lifecycle guards require a consistent read of authoritative facts at a defined decision point. Those later mechanisms are deferred intentionally.

## Predict — Independent progress without imaginary global commit

In the integrated learning explorer, start with Order REQUESTED, RestaurantDecision PENDING and PaymentAttempt NOT_STARTED. Choose a financial authorization branch. The payment model goes through submission to AUTHORIZED while merchant decision remains PENDING. Predict what the customer can safely be told: the local order request is recorded, an illustrative payment authorization state is modeled, and restaurant acceptance is not known. The workflow is **partially progressed**, not successful delivery.

Now change only the merchant decision to REJECTED and payment to REFUND_PENDING after a hypothetical capture. The merchant has rejected the fulfillment obligation, but financial remediation remains unresolved. A UI that says only REJECTED omits the financial risk. A UI that says only REFUNDED is false because the refund outcome is still pending. The correct combined description is conditional: merchant rejection known; captured payment and pending refund are hypothetical model states; final provider effect has not been established in the real system.

Predict before revealing the explanation. The explorer should be deterministic and expose the same correlation constraints as the Java model, not invent authoritative provider callbacks. Use the reset control to compare adjacent histories. Ask what changed locally, whose fact was assumed, and which business obligation remains unresolved. The answer is often a **set of facts and obligations**, not a single order status.

## Understand — Illegal transitions and the unchanged-state contract

For a transition function, illegal action must be explicit. Returning an unchanged state with a rejection reason is not the same as throwing a null pointer or silently ignoring an invalid command. It enables tests to assert three things separately: the action was rejected; the original state and identity were preserved; no success fact was produced. A negative result need not be an exception if the action is a normal business possibility.

The local merchant model rejects a second decision after ACCEPTED or REJECTED. The local payment model rejects 35 out of 42 possible combinations in its deliberately limited state/action matrix. The Order model rejects duplicate RECORD_INTAKE and policy-dependent closure. Exhaustive tables reveal forgotten edges, including illegal attempts from terminal states. They also expose whether a transition's fact label is honest about the model's scope.

However, purity does not automatically mean atomic updates. A pure method may return an accepted decision based on old state while another thread updates the authoritative store. The application needs an enforcement mechanism when writing the result; tests that only invoke the pure function do not provide concurrency guarantees. This is exactly why Lesson 1.1.7 explores guards and postconditions, while later lessons address races and stale state.

Be precise about "state preservation." An immutable record cannot be mutated, so an illegal transition returns the original record. But if a caller wraps it in a mutable repository and writes incorrectly anyway, invariants may still be broken. The test proves local behavior, not every possible adapter behavior.

### Partial order is more informative than one global timestamp

Suppose a merchant accepted a specific basket at its source time M1 and the provider authorized the related attempt at source time P1. The platform receives M1 at local time A2 and P1 at A1. The order A1 < A2 of platform arrivals does not prove P1 occurred before M1. The merchant's and provider's clocks need not be synchronized, and a network can reorder delivery. Even using synchronized clocks does not automatically establish a shared atomic decision; authority and causal correlation still matter.

The platform should preserve separate notions: **source occurrence time** (if trustworthy and defined), **platform observation time**, **operation identity**, **source sequence/version** (if provided), and **correlation to business intention**. A problem report that provides only the most recent `updatedAt` loses too much context to diagnose. When events are delayed or duplicated, the correct question is not "which timestamp is largest?" but "what does each source's evidence actually establish, and does it supersede a prior source decision for the same identity?"

This motivates an event-history or audit model in later work, but today's pure states intentionally do not implement it. The student should be able to draw two plausible histories that yield the same visible UI status: a provider declined an attempt and its response was lost, or the provider captured money and its response was lost. In both cases `SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN` may be the only defensible platform observation. The recovery strategy must discriminate before making stronger claims.

## Understand — Ordering, observation times and competing histories

Suppose a conceptual provider sends AUTHORIZED for PA-10 at 18:01, then merchant M-42 rejects O-10 at 18:02. A refund might be required only if an actual capture occurred; authorization alone may require release or expiry instead. Now suppose the platform observes the merchant rejection at 18:03 and the delayed authorization at 18:04. The latest received message is not necessarily the latest business decision and cannot by itself determine what money moved.

A different failure: an old PA-9 decline arrives after a newer PA-10 authorization. If the platform has one `Order.paymentStatus` field without attempt identity, the late decline can overwrite the newer success. If each attempt has its own identity and state, both facts can coexist. The order-view projection may choose the relevant attempt under explicit rules; it does not erase PA-9's history. Identity decomposition thus precedes reliable event correlation.

For merchant decisions, a delayed ACCEPT observation after REJECT could indicate a revised source decision, an old message, two decision identities, a changed basket, or incorrect correlation. The pure one-shot model refuses a second transition, but a real diagnosis must inspect authoritative revisions and timestamps rather than assume that the local enum knows what occurred. Clock order alone is not a reliable cross-system causality rule.

Unknown means "insufficient correlated evidence," not a fourth synonym for failure. `SUBMITTED_UNKNOWN` keeps that distinction visible. Retrying or reconciling requires provider-specific capabilities, security, idempotency and operations; it is not performed in the current Java slice.

## Understand — The unresolved business policy register constrains model claims

Lesson 1.1.5 intentionally left POL-01–POL-10 unresolved. This remains the authoritative status. The following are examples of decisions **not** licensed by a state-machine class: capture before acceptance versus acceptance before capture; whether merchant acceptance creates a binding obligation; cancellation eligibility when kitchen work starts; refund liability and timing after merchant rejection; what provider evidence counts as refund completion; preparation gate while payment is unknown; who can establish delivery and what proof is sufficient.

A useful discipline is to annotate each diagram edge with a classification. **Implemented and observed** means actual code performed it within a stated boundary. **Pure and simulated** means deterministic behavior under an assumed input. **External authority required** means a real participant would have to attest it. **Policy unresolved** means the business has not approved the edge or its consequences. These labels prevent attractive diagrams from being mistaken for executable business policy.

In the Java model, merchant ACCEPT and REJECT are isolated teaching actions. They do not assert that the real restaurant accepted or that the marketplace has agreed to terminal one-shot semantics. Payment confirmation actions encode the consequence of assuming an authenticated provider observation, but the class never authenticates such an observation. Order CLOSE_UNFULFILLABLE remains rejected because doing otherwise would approve a cross-lifecycle rule. This asymmetry is intentional and should survive code review.

The right next step is not to "fill in the missing transitions" for completeness. It is to resolve policy through accountable stakeholders, define evidence sources, derive the legal transition table and test the committed guarantees. Some decisions may remain unknown throughout this phase. An honest domain model makes that uncertainty visible.

### A discriminating incident reconstruction

Prepare a hypothetical evidence table before rewriting any status:

| Evidence question | Plausible observation | What it distinguishes |
|---|---|---|
| Which OrderId and basket revision? | O-57, revision B1 | Whether merchant decision applies to this purchase |
| Which merchant identity? | M-42, signed rejection for B1 | Real rejection versus stale or wrong restaurant screen |
| Which payment attempt? | PA-10 associated with O-57 | Avoid joining an unrelated operation |
| What was the financial operation? | Capture request with provider ref CAP-55 | Submission versus actual provider capture |
| Was a refund requested? | Refund instruction R-8 | Intent versus completed financial effect |
| What does provider attest? | R-8 accepted for processing; completion unknown | Why REFUNDED is unjustified |
| What did UI display and when? | REFUNDED based on a local action at T4 | Projection error and missing authority check |

The case is not resolved by calling `order.status=REJECTED`: doing so would simply swap one collapsed value for another. Nor is it resolved by making `RefundedOrder` a separate microservice. The meaningful repair is to retain merchant decision evidence separately from provider attempts and refund-operation evidence, then derive a qualified customer-facing summary under approved policy. That can be done in a single deployable when the underlying authoritative sources and business rules are available.

Ask one counterfactual: if the provider actually completed refund R-8 at T5 while the merchant rejection remained true, what should the customer see? Both facts, with their proper scope. A single global status that can display only one has already lost information even on the successful remediation path.

## Diagnose — Why the order says REFUNDED but the merchant says REJECTED

A customer reports a restaurant rejection and an apparently completed refund. Support finds an aggregate `OrderStatus=REFUNDED`, but no preserved payment-attempt history. What can that field prove? Almost nothing about the original capture, which attempt was involved, whether a provider actually completed a refund, or when the restaurant rejected. The data model has collapsed at least three authorities: merchant fulfillment, provider financial operation and platform presentation.

Start by recovering OrderId, RestaurantId, all associated PaymentAttemptIds, provider operation references and timestamps. Confirm the merchant decision from its authoritative source. Query or inspect trusted provider evidence for the actual capture/refund operation and amount/currency. Compare those facts with the projection derivation and display freshness. A UI update based on "refund requested" is different from provider-confirmed completion.

Competing hypotheses include (H1) a refund request was treated as success; (H2) a provider callback was lost; (H3) the refund relates to a different attempt; (H4) a late decline overwrote a newer capture; (H5) the merchant decision belongs to an earlier basket; (H6) a local status update impersonated an external fact. Each requires a different discriminating observation. Avoid declaring the customer compensated until the evidence exists.

The architectural diagnosis is not "add Kafka." It is that the domain representation erased independent identities, sources and lifecycle histories. Even a single-process modular application should separate these dimensions before distributed infrastructure is justified. Once external providers are integrated, their authority remains external regardless of service deployment.

## Defend — Communicate boundaries at Senior and Staff levels

To a junior engineer: "One customer order involves several separate things. Recording the request, a restaurant accepting it, and a payment provider authorizing an attempt are not the same fact. Each can change independently."

To a senior backend engineer: "The transition decision for one entity is a pure function over its current state and a requested action. Invalid transitions preserve identity, original state and fact absence. Cross-lifecycle predicates are derived observations and do not themselves authorize external effects. The application needs separately justified commit/guard logic for real writes."

To a Staff or Principal reviewer: "Decomposition is motivated by business authority and evidence provenance, not deployment topology. We retain a single deployable and explicit local intake. Restaurant and payment models are intentionally illustrative; unresolved POL-01–POL-10 rules prevent us from approving global workflows. A state machine per owner reduces combinatorial coupling but does not eliminate cross-aggregate invariants, stale reads or failure reconciliation."

To a product manager: "The customer can receive an order-request acknowledgement before restaurant acceptance. An authorization is not capture, and a refund request is not a completed refund. We need explicit business decisions before promising cancellation or compensation behavior."

To a nontechnical stakeholder: "The restaurant's ability to make the meal and the financial provider's handling of money are separate. If one succeeds while the other fails, the system must clearly explain what has happened and what remains unresolved."

The best architectural defense is falsifiable: point to a transition table, a rejected path test, a preserved identifier, and a distinct authority source. Avoid claiming that the diagram or local tests prove provider behavior.
