# Lesson 1.1.5 — A Complete Food Ordering Workflow Story

**Cluster 1.1 · Chapter 1 · Type C conceptual analysis + Type D academy learning evolution.**

**Engineering question:** How do responsibilities compose into a customer-visible business outcome?

**Evidence contract:** The real Java 21/Spring Boot application at [ea17135cdc1974d89d78ce78931bb63cc5e9544e](https://github.com/fasiltadesse22/food-ordering-platform-new/tree/ea17135cdc1974d89d78ce78931bb63cc5e9544e) implements order-request intake only. [Previous 30-test Java CI and packaged HTTP smoke](https://github.com/fasiltadesse22/food-ordering-platform-new/actions/runs/42782082917) establish its local baseline. Everything involving merchant acceptance, payment attempts, financial capture, refund, kitchen execution, or delivery is **conceptual**. The [business process map](../../docs/domain/CLUSTER_1_1_LESSON_1_1_5_WORKFLOW_PROCESS_MAP.md) and [POL-01–POL-10 decision register](../../docs/domain/CLUSTER_1_1_LESSON_1_1_5_DECISION_BACKLOG.md) are analytical inputs, not approved business policy.

## Orient — The customer sees one journey; the business has many authorities

Imagine ordering dinner from Cedar Kitchen. Your screen says “Order confirmed.” A restaurant employee says they rejected the basket. Your bank app shows a card debit, but customer support cannot confirm a refund. Did the order succeed, fail, or remain in progress? Every single-word answer is potentially misleading. There may be three independently established facts: an order request recorded by the platform, an explicit restaurant rejection, and a provider-confirmed financial effect. The customer's desire for one answer does not turn these facts into one transaction.

Lessons 1.1.1–1.1.4 established the pieces: actors and decision rights; precise use cases; the separation of commands, decisions, facts, and acknowledgements; and identities with authoritative ownership. This lesson connects those pieces into a *business process*. Its purpose is not to choose microservices, draw an attractive sequence, or add a status enum. Its purpose is to show what must happen for the entire customer promise to become true, and which obligations remain when different participants disagree or complete only part of the work.

An ordinary HTTP 201 from today's Java application means an eligible intake resource was saved in the process-local repository. It does **not** mean a merchant has accepted preparation, a payment provider has authorized or captured money, or a carrier has delivered food. The truthful phrase is “Order request received.” Any stronger statement requires additional evidence. Begin with this question: **Which authority must establish each missing fact before we may tell the customer their outcome is complete?**

## Understand — A business workflow is a composition, not a call chain

A **business goal** is the desired end result: obtaining the meal under agreed commercial terms. A **customer-visible outcome** is the representation delivered to the customer, which must be supported by business facts rather than UI optimism. A **responsibility** assigns an actor accountability for a decision or effect. A **command** expresses requested change; a **decision** grants or rejects that request according to authority and policy; an **established fact** asserts an outcome under a defined source-of-truth boundary. An **event/message** communicates a fact or intention, but its transport and observation do not establish the underlying reality. A **state transition** changes a particular entity's lifecycle; a **workflow milestone** describes progress spanning multiple participant lifecycles. An **acknowledgement** describes processing or receipt of a request, not necessarily its external effect. An **external effect** might include money capture, consumed ingredients, custody handoff, or delivered food. A **completion criterion** says precisely which facts and obligations together justify the end-state promise.

A workflow is therefore a composition of decisions, identities, facts, obligations, dependencies and outcomes across independent responsibilities. A request sequence is only one possible observation of that composition. Even if the implementation were a single synchronous Java method, merchant acceptance and the financial provider's outcome would remain external authorities. Conversely, dividing software into six services would not magically create six independent business authorities: an actor is not a microservice.

Distinguish **intent → permitted decision → established fact → communicated observation**. A customer pressing “Cancel” is intent; a policy-aware cancellation decision is something different; an actual cancellation fact requires that decision and its correct recording; a UI message is downstream evidence at best. All four can diverge temporarily or permanently. The failure of a response to arrive says little about whether its effect occurred.

## Understand — Actors, incentives, decision rights and obligations

**Customer.** The customer chooses restaurant, basket, contact/delivery options and asks to purchase or cancel. Their objective is a meal for a known cost and time. They can assert their own intention and receipt experience; they cannot unilaterally establish restaurant acceptance, financial capture, refund completion, or delivery custody. They need truthful progress updates and clear recourse when facts are missing.

**Ordering platform.** The platform owns the local intake resource, its identity, validation against currently available information, the processing rules it is authorized to enforce, and customer-facing coordination. It can decide whether to record an intake. It can observe and correlate external responses and may enforce approved marketplace policy, but does not gain authority to invent a merchant or provider outcome. Its duty extends to communicating uncertainty and arranging reconciliation, even where no direct technical fix is available.

**Restaurant/merchant.** A merchant decides whether it can commit to fulfilling a specific basket, price and timing under agreed terms. The relevant merchant identity must remain stable across display-name changes. Acceptance creates commercial/operational obligations whose scope must be approved. A rejection does not automatically reverse a provider's completed charge. Restaurant capability is not evidence that the current Java platform contacts real restaurants.

**Kitchen.** Kitchen staff or their authorized operational system establish preparation start, readiness and completion according to merchant workflow. Ingredients and labor are physical effects, not mutable fields. The merchant's acceptance decision and the kitchen's preparation fact are related, but neither is interchangeable with the other.

**Payment provider and financial ledger.** An authenticated provider establishes the outcome of an individual authorization, capture or refund operation under provider-specific identities and settlement rules. Platform submission and provider acknowledgement are distinct from completed effect. An OrderId may relate to several future PaymentAttemptIds and provider operation references. The current Java app has no such integration.

**Delivery or pickup participant.** A courier, restaurant pickup operator or customer may establish distinct custody milestones. “Ready,” “collected,” and “received” are three facts with different proof and potential disputes. A logistics handoff can introduce new obligations without immediately proving customer receipt.

**Support/dispute function.** Support investigates mismatched projections, identities, timestamps, provider responses and compensatory obligations. Support can initiate remediation under policy, but cannot make an unknown financial outcome become known by changing a status. Escalation and human exceptions are business workflow components, not architectural failure by definition.

## Understand — The verified Java boundary and an illustrative full journey

The verified implemented sequence is: **POST /api/orders → PlaceOrderCommand → business decision → immutable Order snapshot → process-local repository save → OrderRequestRecorded → HTTP 201**. The types OrderId, CustomerId and RestaurantId disambiguate local references; PlaceOrderOutcome distinguishes accepted intake from rejection. The returned order remains merchant-decision PENDING and payment NOT_STARTED. An eligible repeated POST can create a distinct order identity because semantic deduplication is not yet implemented. Storage is not durable across process loss, and the raw customer ID is not authentication.

From that explicit boundary onward, the following twelve-stage story is a **hypothetical policy illustration**, not application behavior:

| Stage | Initiator → authority | Fact or decision | Customer-safe interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Basket selected | Customer → customer | Intent and proposed basket | “Your selection” |
| 2. Purchase requested | Customer → platform | PlaceOrderCommand received | “Submitting request” |
| 3. Inputs evaluated | Platform → platform | Eligibility decision or rejection | “We can/cannot record this request” |
| 4. Intake recorded | Platform → local repository | OrderRequestRecorded for OrderId | “Request received; awaiting merchant” |
| 5. Merchant invited | Platform → merchant | Invitation submitted, *not* acceptance | “Awaiting restaurant decision” |
| 6. Merchant decides | Restaurant → merchant | Accepted or Rejected under policy | “Accepted by restaurant” only on evidence |
| 7. Financial operation | Platform request → provider | Authorization/capture result for provider operation | “Authorized” ≠ “charged” ≠ “settled” |
| 8. Preparation starts | Kitchen → kitchen/merchant | PreparationStarted, with physical cost | “Being prepared” only on verified fact |
| 9. Food becomes ready | Kitchen → kitchen/merchant | PreparationCompleted | “Ready for handoff” |
| 10. Custody transferred | Merchant/courier → authorized witnesses | Pickup/custody fact | “Collected by courier” |
| 11. Delivery proved | Courier/recipient → agreed evidence authority | Delivery completion evidence | “Delivered” according to agreed standard |
| 12. Promise closed | Platform projection → customer | Completion claim based on reconciled facts | “Completed” only when necessary obligations met |

For **each** stage, the architect must ask: What triggered the request? Which OrderId, RestaurantId, PaymentAttemptId, provider operation or delivery reference ties the action to the same business intention? Who may decide, and what must be true beforehand? Where does the fact live? Could an acknowledgement be missing? Can a rejection still occur? Is the outcome unknown rather than false? Did a physical or financial effect cross an irreversible boundary? What can the customer safely infer? Answering these questions *before* writing transitions prevents accidental coupling of separate authorities.

The order between stages 6 and 7 is explicitly unsettled (POL-01). One illustrative path accepts restaurant commitment before financial capture; another may authorize or capture before merchant acceptance. The diagram is not a universal sequence. The customer-visible completion milestone likewise depends on delivery proof, commercial policy, and any unresolved financial obligations. “Order completed” can be an operational delivery claim while a financial dispute remains open; do not silently turn one into the other.

### Walk the milestone contracts rather than memorizing their names

**Milestone 1 — customer intent.** The trigger is the customer's selection and explicit purchase action. The input is a basket, merchant choice and delivery expectation. Its authority is restricted to *what the customer requested*. The basket may be stale, prices may have changed, and a customer-selected merchant may not be accepting orders. Therefore the command cannot establish price acceptance or fulfillment availability. A request identifier helps trace this action; it does not confer authorization and is not necessarily a business-order identity. There may be an unknown client outcome if the request is sent but no response arrives.

**Milestone 2 — local validation.** The platform is entitled to check whether the request conforms to the currently implemented intake contract: item references, quantities, a known fixture merchant, and any locally enforceable rule. The result can be permitted intake or a precise rejection. A valid command shape is insufficient if a business guard rejects the specific proposed basket. If the backend returns an ordinary business rejection, no successful intake fact should be claimed. If transport breaks mid-processing, that is not automatically a business rejection.

**Milestone 3 — recording the request.** The platform constructs an immutable snapshot and records it in a process-local repository. The application can then construct OrderRequestRecorded and return an HTTP resource location. The authoritative scope is extremely narrow: that specific running application process at the observed checkpoint. The identity is OrderId, correlated to CustomerId and RestaurantId. The local write is not a durable cross-process commit, and an acknowledgement failure can leave a caller unable to distinguish a recorded intake from an unrecorded one. This is the only milestone actually implemented in the present Java baseline.

**Milestone 4 — merchant decision eligibility.** A platform may determine that an intake is ready to be presented to a merchant, but this is *not* merchant acceptance. Under a future integration, it would need an identity-preserving merchant request, a response deadline and an explicit distinction among not sent, sent, observed, rejected, accepted and unknown. A queueing acknowledgement would not answer whether the restaurant can prepare the meal. If the recipient does not respond, “pending” can be honest; “accepted” cannot.

**Milestone 5 — restaurant commitment.** The merchant's decision may depend on stock, kitchen capacity, operating hours, promised preparation time and contractual terms. The request must be the correct merchant's and correspond to the same basket and price version. A legitimate acceptance establishes a promise with operational consequences. A rejection ends one fulfillment possibility but may leave financial cleanup. A lost response introduces uncertainty about the merchant decision; reading the platform's local “pending” field is insufficient to overrule a merchant-side accepted fact.

**Milestone 6 — financial outcome.** A platform may initiate an attempt, but the provider or authoritative financial ledger determines authorization, capture, reversal and refund effects. These require a provider-specific operation identifier, amount/currency and a relationship to the business order. The requested amount must correspond to the basket and selected policy, not merely to a stale UI quotation. A transport-level success can mean that the provider accepted a request for processing; it does not necessarily establish a financial effect. If the outcome is unknown, recovery must discriminate rather than retry indiscriminately.

**Milestone 7 — preparation permission and beginning.** Depending on unresolved policy, preparation may be gated by merchant acceptance, payment authorization/capture, or another guarantee. The kitchen actor creates evidence that work physically began. That fact is neither equivalent to merchant acceptance nor reversible by rolling back a database field. Cancellation concurrently requested with preparation may be ineligible, eligible with compensation, or escalated; the final policy has not been approved.

**Milestone 8 — preparation ready.** A ready notification means the meal has been prepared under kitchen authority, not that any courier is available. Cold-chain or food-safety constraints, pickup deadline and abandonment risk may now matter. “Ready” must not be projected as “collected” or “delivered.” If kitchen readiness was observed late, the display can lag without the kitchen fact becoming false.

**Milestone 9 — handoff.** Assignment of a carrier does not prove pickup. A custody-transfer event needs the responsible participants, order/package identity, timestamp and agreed evidence. A carrier collecting the wrong order can produce a technically well-formed handoff event that violates the business contract. The fact may be disputed; verification and escalation are then part of the workflow.

**Milestone 10 — completion evidence.** Under a hypothetical courier policy, delivery proof could include a recipient acknowledgement or other agreed evidence. The policy must determine what constitutes sufficient proof, how to handle failed delivery, wrong recipients and missing acknowledgement, and whether delivery completion closes all obligations or merely fulfillment. An operational “delivered” status should not secretly imply that a financial dispute is resolved.

**Milestone 11 — customer projection.** The platform combines the facts it has *observed*, with their source identities and freshness, into language the customer can rely on. This projection can be incomplete or stale. It must preserve uncertainty rather than promote missing information to success. The platform owns the wording and its own observation history, not the authority of the merchant, kitchen, provider or courier.

Across these contracts, the same question repeats: **what exactly is new, who had power to make it true, and what remains unresolved after it occurred?** A well-designed sequence diagram follows these answers; it cannot supply them by itself.

## Understand — Compare payment-first and acceptance-first policies

**Payment-first** may authorize or capture funds before asking the merchant to commit. It can reduce one risk—sending an unpayable basket to the kitchen—but creates another: a financially successful operation followed by merchant rejection. If authorization-only is used, it may be releasable under provider rules; if capture occurred, a separate refund or reversal workflow is needed. Even when the refund is commercially owed, provider settlement timing and consumer-visible posting can remain uncertain. Payment-first therefore requires an explicit reconciliation and customer communication policy.

**Acceptance-first** obtains restaurant commitment before requesting financial action. It can avoid charging for merchant-rejected requests, but creates a window in which the merchant has committed and financial approval may fail or time out. A kitchen that starts during that window may consume resources before commercial protection is established. The policy needs an explicit preparation gate, acceptance expiry and responsibility for failed or ambiguous payment.

**Authorize-first, accept, capture-later** is another candidate, but its eligibility, expiry, partial capture and failed capture still require provider/merchant contracts. The blueprint does not approve one ordering. The exercise is to compare exposure rather than quietly select an option. Neither branch proves distributed atomicity, and adding a Saga would not settle who bears financial loss.

### The business obligation ledger of a normal path

Consider a hypothetical order priced at 300 units of currency. We use invented identities O-101, M-42, PA-1 and CAP-1 solely for analysis; the currency and amount are illustrative, not current Java test data. The customer submits the basket, the platform records O-101, and the restaurant decides it can prepare the exact basket. At this point there is a merchant obligation but no proof of money movement. Under the assumed acceptance-first policy, the platform then requests capture linked to PA-1. Only provider evidence for CAP-1 establishes the captured effect. It must not be inferred from a log saying “capture requested.”

Before the kitchen begins, the platform may have a local projection of **merchant accepted / financial captured / preparation not started**. These are independent dimensions. When preparation starts, the financial capture does not become “more captured”; a separate kitchen fact and physical liability are created. When food is ready, the kitchen obligation shifts toward handoff, but there is no delivery success. When pickup is confirmed, custody changes; again, that does not imply the recipient got the meal. Only after delivery evidence and any other policy-mandated conditions can the platform close the customer-facing fulfillment journey.

Try deleting a single observation. Suppose CAP-1 actually succeeded at the provider but the acknowledgement did not arrive. From the provider's perspective, financial success is known; from the platform's perspective it is unknown. The platform should not change the authoritative financial fact merely because its local read model is incomplete. A reconciliation request may eventually recover evidence. The original sequence might be physically identical to the fully observed case, but the defensible UI copy and next operational action differ.

The lesson therefore has two success criteria: *the business effects actually occurred under authority*, and *the platform has enough correlated evidence to present the agreed customer promise*. One does not automatically prove the other. In later lessons, these become separate state machines and recovery conditions rather than one all-purpose SUCCESS enum.

## Understand — Scenario A: a successful but not atomic customer journey

*Conceptual illustration; assumptions: merchant acceptance is obtained before capture, preparation waits for verified financial capture, delivery is courier-based, and platform completion requires evidence of handoff and delivery.* At T1 the customer's request is locally recorded: only intake is established. At T2 merchant M-42 accepts basket B under its responsibility. At T3 the provider confirms capture C-77 for PaymentAttempt P-9. At T4 the kitchen begins preparing; this crosses a physical commitment boundary. At T5 preparation ends; at T6 the carrier acknowledges possession; at T7 delivery is evidenced according to the proposed contract. At T8 the customer projection may truthfully say “Delivered” and the appropriate order-level completion condition may be met.

If the merchant acknowledgement is delayed, payment remains unstarted; if the provider capture response is lost, preparation must follow the proposed unknown-outcome policy rather than assume success; if a courier collects but does not deliver, custody transfer exists without completion. A happy path is a *path through explicit gates*, not evidence that all actions share one commit. Write down why each business authority's independent fact was necessary.

### Step-by-step failure investigation

At 19:00 an illustrative payment-first platform records intake O-202. At 19:00:02 it submits capture CAP-202 under attempt PA-202. At 19:00:03 the provider records a successful capture. At 19:00:04 the merchant rejects because the kitchen is out of stock. At 19:00:05 the customer refreshes and sees “Order rejected.” Four independent questions arise. Was capture authorized or actually completed? Is the merchant's rejection authentic and associated with O-202? Has an authorized business policy decided who owes what? Has the provider confirmed a specific refund against CAP-202? The fourth cannot be answered from the first three.

Imagine support immediately changes an order field to REFUNDED. This removes neither the provider's captured amount nor the provider obligation. Worse, it can prevent operators from noticing that the money was never returned. A more precise remediation narrative has: merchant rejection; policy-based refund eligibility; refund instruction; provider acceptance/processing; final refund confirmation; possible downstream customer account posting. Each step requires identity and evidence. Even if every technical request returns HTTP 200, the result may remain unknown until confirmed at the authoritative financial source.

Now vary only the timing. If merchant rejection arrives before capture is initiated, the platform may avoid initiating capture altogether. If authorization succeeded but capture never occurred, the necessary remediation might be authorization release or expiry rather than a refund of captured funds. If capture happened but the acknowledgement was lost, retrying capture could cause a second effect without idempotency protection. If a refund was issued but its acknowledgement was lost, retrying refund poses the analogous problem. Each is a different branch, not one generic “payment failed” exception.

The immediate support objective is to prevent a misleading customer promise and preserve evidence for financial reconciliation. The long-term engineering objective is to identify the actual policy, provider contract, recovery windows, observability and duplicate-effect protection justified by this scenario. Those later mechanisms should not be invented as existing features in today's Java baseline.

## Understand — Scenario B: paid but restaurant rejected

*Conceptual illustration under a payment-before-merchant policy.* At T1 the platform records intake O-51. At T2 the provider confirms capture C-88 tied to attempt P-3. At T3 the merchant rejects O-51 because an ingredient is unavailable. The facts are not contradictory: one actor established a financial effect; another rejected preparation. An order-level projection that says only “Rejected” hides the captured money. A projection that says “Paid” hides the rejected fulfillment.

Cancellation or rejection does not rewind the external financial ledger. An approved refund eligibility decision (POL-05) is not refund submission; refund submission is not provider acceptance; acceptance for processing is not refund completion; a provider-confirmed refund may still differ from when a customer sees funds posted. The support workflow must correlate capture and refund operation identities, communicate pending/unknown outcome accurately, and avoid issuing a second refund blindly after an ambiguous timeout.

Safe wording: “The restaurant cannot fulfill your order. We have recorded the payment outcome and are resolving the required financial action. Refund completion has not yet been confirmed.” This statement presupposes capture evidence and a known reconciliation process; otherwise say the payment status is being verified. What happens next depends on POL-05 through POL-07, none of which is approved in this lesson.

### Why cancellation is a decision race, not a button handler

Suppose restaurant M-42 accepts O-303 at 18:03:00; the customer presses Cancel at 18:03:01; kitchen staff begin work at 18:03:02. The platform receives the cancellation command at 18:03:03, after kitchen start in wall-clock time. If the hypothetical rule is “allow cancellation until preparation starts,” the business must decide whether eligibility is evaluated when the customer pressed the button, when the platform received it, when the merchant received it, or against an authoritative recorded kitchen-start fact. Those are genuinely different policies and can yield different outcomes.

A local guard that checks a cached “not preparing” flag at 18:03:03 may be stale. It can approve cancellation even though the kitchen has already started. Conversely, a merchant screen may show “Preparing” because of an earlier observation while the kitchen fact was never established for this order. The architect must map the exact source, state version, identity and ordering before deciding whether any result was valid.

What about a cancellation accepted at 18:03:01 followed by a delayed preparation-start message at 18:03:04? That message may have been created before acceptance of cancellation, or the kitchen may have begun work despite cancellation. Observed arrival order alone cannot establish causality. The system will eventually require ordering contracts and conflict resolution; Lesson 1.1.13 later studies concurrency interleavings and Lesson 1.1.15 later studies stale state. Here our job is to identify the business contradiction that must be prevented.

Consider the commercially distinct outcomes: cancellation denied with explanation; cancellation approved with no fee; approved with an approved preparation fee; customer-service exception; refund pending despite cancellation approved; delivery already handed off and subject to return/dispute rather than ordinary cancellation. None may be selected automatically without POL-03 and POL-04. The safest UI flow is **request received → decision pending → authorized decision observed → financial resolution tracked separately**.

## Understand — Scenario C: accepted then cancellation requested

*Conceptual illustration.* At T1 merchant acceptance creates an obligation; at T2 the customer presses Cancel. That press establishes **CancellationRequested**, not **OrderCancelled**. A policy and an authorized decision maker must determine whether cancellation is permitted and which other participant obligations it affects. A successful cancellation decision must be recorded as a distinct fact. If an acknowledgement to the customer is lost, the customer may not know whether it was accepted.

Compare five windows. **Before preparation** may permit cancellation with low operational cost but still require financial release. **Just after acceptance** may have an accepted merchant obligation even before an external effect. **After preparation starts** creates consumed ingredients and labor, changing refund/compensation alternatives. **After capture** introduces a separate financial operation even where cancellation is permitted. **After pickup or delivery handoff** custody and return/dispute policies replace simplistic cancellation semantics.

A customer-visible “Cancellation requested” status is appropriate while the decision is pending. “Cancelled” requires decision evidence. “Refund complete” requires its own provider evidence. Neither can be established by one button click. POL-03 and POL-04 determine eligibility and consequences; a UI must not preapprove them.

## Understand — Scenario D: preparation already started

Preparation is a physically consequential milestone. Once a kitchen opens ingredients, cooks food, or allocates staff time, the material world has changed. Setting an order field back to a prior value does not restore consumed ingredients, elapsed labor, food safety, reserved capacity or waste. This is why the reversible/irreversible distinction has both technical and commercial dimensions.

Imagine acceptance at 18:00 and preparation started at 18:03. A cancellation request arrives at 18:04; the customer sees a spinner, while the kitchen has already consumed ingredients. The platform could record the request without being entitled to declare it approved. Alternative policies could permit cancellation with merchant compensation, deny cancellation after a cutoff, issue partial refund, or escalate an exception. Each alternative changes both the customer promise and an actor's economic exposure.

The exact moment of irreversible commitment may differ between restaurants or menu items. The policy must identify who attests preparation start and which time/identity is authoritative; relying on a delayed dashboard observation can let a stale cancellation decision ignore a real kitchen action. This business problem motivates later state/version guards and coordination experiments, but this Type C lesson does **not** implement Saga or compensation.

## Understand — Scenario E: refund requested without confirmation

*Conceptual illustration.* A support agent approves eligibility for refund R-12 on a captured provider operation. The platform sends the refund request and receives “accepted for processing.” A network failure occurs before final status arrives. The system has evidence of a **request**, perhaps a provider acceptance-for-processing, and an **unknown completion**. No one may infer financial completion merely because the outbound HTTP call succeeded.

Distinguish a refund-request identity from the original payment-attempt identity; preserve the provider namespace and amount/currency. A provider may reject, process later, send a delayed callback or return an ambiguous acknowledgement. Repeating blindly could duplicate a monetary effect if provider idempotency semantics are absent. The exact recovery mechanism belongs in a later lesson and requires actual provider capabilities and approved policies.

The customer needs two truths simultaneously: the merchant cannot fulfill the meal, and the financial obligation is **not yet confirmed resolved**. Avoid saying either “No refund” or “Refunded” where the outcome is unknown. Financial receipt, ledger settlement and customer account posting may be separate milestones.

## Understand — Scenario F: partial completion and conflicting screens

*Conceptual mixed outcome.* O-73 has been recorded. Restaurant M-42 accepted it. The payment attempt was submitted, but the acknowledgement was lost. The kitchen began preparing under a hypothetical permissive policy. The customer requests cancellation. The merchant dashboard, refreshed recently, shows “Preparing”; the customer dashboard has an older “Awaiting acceptance” projection. The platform cannot collapse this into “In progress” without losing decisive unknowns.

Build an evidence ledger: the local intake fact is known; restaurant acceptance is known only with verified merchant source; provider payment outcome is unknown; preparation has an asserted kitchen milestone requiring identity/timestamp validation; cancellation decision is unresolved; customer projection is stale. At each row record the authority, observation time and possible next action. The existence of a lost acknowledgement tells us nothing definitive about provider effect.

A credible resolution approach investigates the correlated provider operation and merchant/kitchen sources before changing projections. A policy may block fresh preparation while payment is unknown; if preparation already occurred, an exception process may be needed. The customer should see a specific non-misleading message describing uncertainty and responsible next step, not a fabricated aggregate success.

### Three timelines that look the same from one screen

In **History A**, the platform sends capture PA-4, the provider rejects it, and the provider rejection response is lost. In **History B**, the platform sends PA-4, the provider captures money, and the success response is lost. In **History C**, the platform fails before it ever sends PA-4. Immediately afterward the platform might observe exactly the same thing in all three histories: no provider response. Therefore “no response” is observational evidence of neither success nor failure. Distinguishing the histories requires a correlated provider query, callback, transaction journal or other authoritative mechanism that the actual provider supports.

There are two different clocks in play: the moment something happened and the moment it became visible to a particular observer. A message can be observed after another message whose underlying business fact occurred later. If a merchant rejects at 18:00 and a delayed acceptance observation arrives at 18:01, the system must examine whether acceptance/rejection are mutually exclusive for the same decision identity and whether the merchant supplied a version or revision. A naive last-arrival-wins update may rewrite authoritative history incorrectly.

Partial completion also has an *economic* dimension. A payment operation can complete while the restaurant obligation fails. A kitchen can expend labor while the cancellation decision remains unresolved. A courier can take custody while the customer's address becomes unreachable. These are not just temporary consistency anomalies: money, time and physical goods may have changed irreversibly.

A robust incident account therefore needs three ledgers: **business facts** (what authorities established), **observations** (what the platform actually received and when), and **obligations** (what someone must still decide or repair). A single “order status” is a useful derived summary for customers only if it does not conceal the unresolved dimensions. In the current Java application, no distributed observer or payment ledger exists; the three-ledger model is conceptual instruction for later engineering.

## Understand — Timing, partial completion and ambiguous remote results

Consider this illustrative timeline: T1 OrderRequestRecorded; T2 payment submission; T3 merchant rejection; T4 provider response still unknown; T5 the customer reads “Rejected.” What does T5 prove? At most, a merchant result was projected. It does not prove no money moved at T2. The response may be late, lost, out of order or not yet reconciled. The provider could have rejected, authorized, captured, or not processed the request; only properly correlated authoritative evidence can narrow these possibilities.

Different clocks are a further complication. A provider's operation timestamp, a merchant's decision timestamp and the platform's receipt time have different causal meanings. Message arrival order does not necessarily equal business decision order. A delayed “Accepted” observation should not blindly overwrite a later authoritative “Rejected” decision, but deciding which is later may require source sequence/version and identity evidence rather than wall-clock order alone.

Even “exactly one order” cannot be inferred from one customer click. A retry may produce a second eligible POST in the current Java baseline. Future semantic deduplication needs a defined identity for the business intention and a separately tested enforcement mechanism. We name this risk here; detailed idempotency and distributed retries are later topics.

### A receipt taxonomy for support and product language

| Claimed receipt | Narrow evidence | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Intake acknowledgement | Platform saved request in a specific scope | Merchant acceptance, payment, durability |
| Merchant confirmation | Authorized merchant accepted a defined basket | Capture, kitchen completion or delivery |
| Payment authorization | Provider reserved or approved potential funds under provider rules | Actual capture or settlement |
| Payment capture | Provider recorded a capture for an operation | Refund, settlement finality, merchant acceptance |
| Settlement statement | Applicable financial ledger reports settlement | Customer received food |
| Refund request receipt | Refund request was recorded or submitted | Refund completed |
| Refund-processing acknowledgement | Provider accepted work for processing | Provider effect or customer posting |
| Refund confirmation | Provider establishes actual refund outcome | Instant cardholder posting in every bank |
| Kitchen readiness | Restaurant says food is ready | Custody transfer to courier |
| Pickup receipt | Carrier/merchant attests handoff | Correct delivery to recipient |
| Delivery evidence | Agreed delivery authority attests completion | Resolution of every financial complaint |

Why is the distinction operationally important? Imagine a screenshot of “Payment received” from a storefront. It may reflect a request accepted by an internal gateway rather than a provider-confirmed capture. The screenshot cannot by itself determine whether a captured amount exists. A support agent needs the provider operation reference, the amount/currency, the exact operation type, timestamp, authorization and capture relationships and the reconciliation outcome. Similarly, the string “refund processed” may mean “we submitted it” or “the provider completed it”; product language must choose one meaning and stay consistent.

The strongest safe UX wording is specific about stage and source. If an action is locally recorded but externally unknown, say so. Do not convert uncertainty into a negative fact (“you were not charged”) or a positive fact (“your refund has arrived”). In regulated payment settings, even accurate financial terms may carry jurisdiction-specific meaning; this exercise deliberately leaves those legal and provider commitments to legitimate decision authorities.

## Understand — Receipts and promises: what exactly was acknowledged?

The word *receipt* is dangerously overloaded. A platform intake acknowledgement confirms local recording under the stated process-local scope. Merchant order confirmation requires a merchant acceptance fact. A provider authorization permits potential capture subject to financial rules; it is not capture. Capture is not necessarily final settlement. A refund submission or provider queue acknowledgement is not provider-confirmed refund completion. A restaurant “ready” notification is not courier custody; a courier pickup receipt is not delivery to the correct recipient.

Every receipt must identify **what fact it asserts, who attests it, the operation identity, amount/currency where applicable, event time and evidence source**. A screenshot of “Order confirmed” may be a stale UI assertion. It is not automatically a merchant or provider receipt. For a user-visible design, prefer verbs with scoped evidence: “Request received,” “Restaurant accepted,” “Payment authorized,” “Payment captured,” “Refund processing,” “Refund confirmed,” “Ready for pickup,” “Collected,” and “Delivered.” Make ambiguous or unknown situations visible rather than hiding them behind green badges.

The current Java 201 response is an intake-resource outcome. It cannot truthfully be presented as paid, restaurant-accepted or delivered. Marketing wording can violate a business promise even when the controller is technically correct. That is a correctness problem, not merely UX copyediting.

### Custody is a separate lifecycle, not a suffix on kitchen state

Consider a prepared order with a valid kitchen-ready fact at 19:10. At 19:11 the dispatch system assigns courier C-3, but C-3 never appears. If the order status says “Out for delivery” merely because assignment succeeded, the customer receives a false prediction about custody. If C-3 arrives and collects the wrong bag, a pickup event without package identity does not prove custody of this customer's food. If C-3 collects the correct bag but leaves it at the wrong address, the pickup fact remains true while delivery completion is contested.

These failures require evidence beyond a single timestamp. A hypothetical handoff record might connect order/package identity, merchant site, courier identity, observation source, proof method and custody acceptance. A delivery-completion policy might distinguish recipient acceptance, photo proof, one-time code, unattended drop-off, or customer pickup. Each option imposes privacy, fraud, accessibility and dispute considerations, none of which can be settled by picking a new HTTP status.

Now consider food abandonment. The kitchen completed its work, no courier was available, the food became unsafe for delivery, and the customer still wants dinner. The business must decide whether the platform owes replacement, refund, merchant compensation or an exception path. A technical retry of “assign courier” may no longer solve the original business goal, because the perishable goods have aged. This illustrates why a workflow is an obligation system: once an external effect occurs, recovering a customer outcome may require a different action rather than a replay of the same command.

## Understand — Delivery handoff is not the end of preparation

The kitchen may mark food “Ready” while no courier has been assigned. Assignment itself does not prove pickup. A carrier can acknowledge collecting food without proving it reached the customer. A delivery claim may refer to the wrong recipient, address, or package. These are distinct identities, custody changes, and sources of evidence.

Under a conceptual courier policy, restaurant readiness is a merchant/kitchen fact; transfer of possession requires a signed or otherwise attributable handoff; delivery requires agreed completion evidence. For customer pickup, the authorized recipient may establish collection instead of a courier. Failure cases include no carrier available, late pickup, wrong package, incorrect recipient, disputed drop-off, returns and abandoned food. Each case may create obligations: replacement, reimbursement, investigation, cost allocation and customer communication.

A “delivery completed” notification with only “left restaurant” evidence is a false promise. The business must approve what counts as proof and who bears loss when it is contested (POL-09). This lesson specifies evidence needs but does not build logistics software.

## Explore — State and authority overlay: independent facts, one customer projection

An order request has OrderId and a platform-local authority. Merchant acceptance and rejection have restaurant decision identity and merchant authority. Financial operation states belong to the provider's operation/ledger and may involve multiple attempts. Kitchen milestones belong to authorized staff and operational records. Custody and delivery belong to the agreed handoff participants. Refund eligibility may belong to marketplace policy while completed financial effect belongs to the provider. A derived order screen combines observations; it is **not a common mutable source of truth**.

For every milestone, record: actor; fact; stable identity; scope of authority; when the platform last observed it; whether the underlying effect is known, unknown or disputed; whether it is currently implemented; and whether it crosses a financial or physical effect boundary. The companion overlay should make gaps and unimplemented sources conspicuous. When two facts seem to disagree, first ask whether they describe the same operation and same point in time.

## Diagnose — Customer confirmed, merchant rejected, payment receipt present

Start with the apparent contradiction but *do not choose a winning screen*. First obtain the exact OrderId and merchant identity; identify all relevant payment attempts and external operation references; obtain the raw merchant decision and its source/version; classify the receipt as authorization, capture, settlement, or merely payment-request acceptance. Read the customer's projection derivation and freshness. Collect both source event times and platform observation times.

Competing hypotheses include: (H1) the UI mistakenly treats intake as confirmation; (H2) the merchant rejected after the projection was populated; (H3) the receipt is authorization only; (H4) it belongs to a different attempt; (H5) a delayed event overwrote newer observation; (H6) merchant aliases joined the wrong restaurant; (H7) a refund was submitted but not confirmed; (H8) the platform never received the merchant rejection. For each hypothesis define the minimum discriminating observation: raw intake contract for H1; source decision and projection timestamps for H2; provider operation type for H3; attempt correlation for H4; source sequence/version for H5; canonical merchant identity for H6; confirmed refund operation result for H7; merchant-versus-platform delivery evidence for H8.

A safe interim customer message avoids “confirmed,” “not charged,” and “refunded” without their corresponding evidence. Escalation may be appropriate while identities and financial outcomes are reconciled. This incident is conceptual in the current Java project; the lesson is about *how to investigate*, not a claim that this incident occurred in production.

## Defend — Explain the design to five audiences

**Junior:** “We recorded your request, but the restaurant and payment provider make separate decisions. We cannot call the entire order successful until we have their evidence.”

**Senior backend:** Define the command boundary, local postcondition, identity continuity, independent external operation IDs, rejection versus technical uncertainty, and the residual obligations caused by partial completion. Derive acceptance criteria that test each causal edge, not only a final status.

**Staff/Principal:** Defend the absence of one global atomic transaction; explain the projection as non-authoritative; compare payment-first and acceptance-first exposure; identify unapproved POL decisions; show where later durability, idempotency, concurrency controls and recovery mechanisms might become justified. Explain why splitting services or adopting Kafka/Saga now would add coordination machinery without settling the underlying commercial policy.

**Engineering manager:** Clearly separate implemented intake from conceptual merchant/payment/fulfillment stages. Explain decision dependencies and the engineering risk of implementing ambiguous cancellation/refund promises. Recommend business-policy resolution before committing lifecycle rules or integrations.

**Nontechnical stakeholder:** “The restaurant declined the meal after a payment operation. We are verifying the payment and any refund; we will not claim the money has been returned until the financial provider confirms it.” Make responsibility and next action understandable without guaranteeing an unverified timeframe.
