# Lesson 1.1.1 — Actors, Goals, Responsibilities and Capabilities

> **Status:** Full authored teaching lesson. **Track:** Conceptual/domain-discovery (Type C). **Authoritative contract:** Master Roadmap V1.0 and Cluster 1.1 Blueprint V0.1. **Business policies:** Explicit teaching assumptions pending stakeholder validation. Nothing here demonstrates an implemented payment provider or a verified Java workflow.

## Orient — Start with the business, not the architecture

Imagine a customer ordering dinner from Cedar Kitchen through a Food Ordering Platform. The customer chooses items, requests delivery, and sees “Your order is placed.” The restaurant discovers it cannot prepare one dish. At roughly the same time, an external payment provider may have approved an authorization request. A support agent receives an angry message: “You charged me for food the restaurant rejected.”

A diagram with boxes named Order Service, Payment Service and Restaurant Service does not tell us whose decision was valid, whether money actually moved, or what the customer is owed. Before making a service diagram, we must ask: **Which business actors have goals and obligations? Who can request an action? Who may approve it? Who can truthfully attest to its outcome? Who must repair the customer experience?**

The engineering question is not “What endpoints will we create?” It is **“Who participates in a business outcome, and whose goal does each operation serve?”** An actor model is valuable because it allows us to detect missing decisions before those omissions become ambiguous APIs, overwritten state, double effects, and support incidents.

Trace a simple journey in natural language. A customer asks to purchase a meal; the restaurant decides whether it can fulfill that meal; a payment provider decides whether a specific financial operation succeeded; kitchen staff prepare the food when permitted; a delivery participant performs the handoff; platform operations take accountability for coordinating the experience and disputes. Those are **business responsibilities**. They do not imply six microservices, six databases, or even six software components.

**What to notice before using the diagrams:** the customer can request cancellation but may not decide the cancellation policy; the platform can display “paid” but cannot invent an external provider's confirmed financial fact; the restaurant can reject fulfillment but cannot falsify an already confirmed provider authorization. The distinction between **intent, decision authority, business fact, and observation** governs the entire course.

Try to reconstruct this story without nouns ending in “Service.” If you cannot, your software decomposition is premature.

### Your first prediction

Suppose the restaurant rejects an order while the payment provider has a successful charge. Is “order rejected” the same fact as “payment reversed”? No. Different authorities control separate facts. A customer-visible resolution may require a **refund request and later provider confirmation**. This is a business distinction before it becomes a distributed transaction lesson.

### Definition of the learning boundary

We will model actors, roles, capabilities, incentive tensions, authority, and cross-actor collaboration in depth. Detailed command/event semantics, aggregate boundaries, concurrency enforcement, database transactions, Sagas, and provider reconciliation belong to later lessons. We may preview the *business questions* they raise without pretending to solve their implementation now.

## Understand — Actor, stakeholder, role, entity, and service are not synonyms

An **actor** is a person, organization, automated external participant, or other party occupying a role in an interaction and pursuing an observable outcome. Actor is a *modeling viewpoint*, not a Java class or a network process. The same individual may act as a customer in one use case and as restaurant staff in another; conversely, one organization may supply multiple actors because different people hold different decision rights.

A **stakeholder** has an interest in a system or its consequences, even if they do not directly operate it. A regulator interested in payment record retention, a restaurant owner concerned about margins, and a customer-care manager who reports on complaint rates may all be stakeholders. Some are also active actors; others are not. Confusing these concepts causes requirements capture to overfit to whoever clicks the UI.

A **role** expresses the capacity in which someone acts: customer, restaurant manager, preparer, courier, support agent, finance reviewer. A role is a modeling category used to explain permissions and responsibility. It is not identical to a login account or static user table. A real human can hold multiple roles, and a role can be occupied by many people or automated agents subject to appropriate policy.

A **domain entity** represents a business thing with persistent identity such as Order, Restaurant, PaymentAttempt, DeliveryAssignment, or RefundCase. “Restaurant” is ambiguous: a merchant company may be a stakeholder; a restaurant employee is an actor; a Restaurant record is a domain entity; a restaurant management application is software. These distinctions matter because an entity record cannot approve a refund and an API cannot decide who should approve one. People and organizations make the policy; software enforces a defined slice of it.

A **software component** is an implementation unit (controller, module, process, integration adapter, task runner). A **service** can mean an organizational capability, application service method, or independently deployed network endpoint. When we say “Payment Service owns payment,” we have answered nothing until we specify whether “owns” means implementing a UI, deciding to request authorization, recording a provider response, or being the final authority over settlement.

**Counterexample:** “The database decides whether an order can be accepted because the status column changes.” The database can enforce a constraint or transaction that the application instructs it to enforce. It does not invent the restaurant's capacity policy or decide which human can override stock levels. Authority originates in business policy and responsibility, then is implemented through explicit control boundaries.

**Modeling test:** For each noun, ask whether it represents a **party with a goal**, **capacity/permission**, **business object**, **organizational requirement**, or **implementation**. If your diagram mixes these without labels, a design reviewer can accidentally infer that a service deployment is a business ownership boundary.

## Understand — Goals, incentives, and opposing forces

A goal is an outcome an actor seeks. An incentive explains why the actor favors one action over another. A responsibility is an obligation to perform or account for behavior. A capability is a stable ability the business must provide. These four are related, but a goal is not an API; an incentive is not a security permission; a responsibility is not a process name.

The **customer** wants correct items, a known total, acceptable waiting time, predictable cancellation terms, and no unfair financial loss. When a meal is delayed, the customer may prefer cancellation even when the restaurant has already paid for ingredients.

The **restaurant operator** wants operational control, accurate availability, profit after platform fees, feasible prep estimates, and the ability to reject orders it cannot fulfill. The restaurant's incentive to limit late cancellations may conflict with the customer's expectation of flexibility. This disagreement is a policy question, not evidence of a technical race.

**Kitchen staff** want a workable sequence of tickets and clarity that each preparation request is authorized. They may begin work after a manager accepts; they should not be forced to infer payment settlement from a green icon whose source is unknown. **Delivery participants** want reliable pickup readiness, dispatch coordination, safety and accurate proof of handoff. A driver's “delivered” claim may need a separate dispute path; merely displaying it is not irrefutable proof.

The **external payment provider** is authoritative for the outcomes of the financial operations it actually processes. It may reject, authorize, capture, reverse, refund or return an unknown/pending response depending on the product contract. Our platform can initiate a financial request and store evidence of the response; it cannot truthfully turn “timeout” into “declined” unless the provider's semantics justify that conclusion.

**Platform operations and support** are accountable for the integrated customer experience: requests, escalations, fraud controls, dispute routing and communication. They may have permission to *initiate* a refund or to waive a fee within policy. They do not own the provider's completed refund fact. **Finance, risk, and compliance stakeholders** may demand audit evidence, reason codes, limits, segregation of duties and reconciled totals; they influence capability requirements whether or not they ever use the restaurant UI.

A system-design interview will often present “the customer should always be able to cancel” and “the restaurant must never lose money after cooking begins.” Both are legitimate goals, but they can conflict. An experienced engineer does not pick a technology to make the conflict disappear. They ask which party carries the cost, which milestone closes the cancellation window, what override mechanism exists, and which outcome can be independently verified.

### An explicit trade-off to carry forward

A customer-friendly cancellation window may increase merchant cost; a strict merchant-protective window may increase support escalations and abandonments. We have no measured product data here. The correct next step is to record **policy alternatives and the experiment or stakeholder decision needed**, not manufacture a one-size-fits-all guarantee.

## Understand — Capabilities, use cases, and responsibility boundaries

A **business capability** is something the enterprise must be able to do reliably, regardless of which team, application, or vendor implements it. “Manage ordering,” “decide fulfillment,” “manage financial obligations,” “prepare food,” “coordinate delivery,” and “resolve disputes” are capabilities. They survive organizational restructuring and can span multiple teams or applications.

A **use case** is a goal-driven interaction with a defined trigger and observable success or rejection outcome: “Customer places an order”; “Restaurant rejects an unavailable item”; “Support initiates refund review.” A use case may exercise several capabilities. A single capability can support many use cases. For example, financial obligation handling affects payment initiation, charge resolution, partial refunds, and dispute follow-up.

A **responsibility** is a narrower obligation or decision-right assignment inside a capability. Restaurant staff decide whether the kitchen can fulfill the requested basket; the platform records the restaurant's answer and presents it to the customer; payment providers determine outcomes for their own payment operation. This division prevents a platform screen from being mistaken for the authority of a bank or a merchant.

Use a **capability ownership map** to write five entries per capability: desired business outcome; accountable organization/role; initiating actors; authoritative decision-maker or external fact source; and observers/consumers. Without those entries, a label such as “Refunding” can hide three distinct decisions: whether policy grants a refund, who submits the request, and whether the provider confirms transfer of funds.

Notice the difference between **accountability** and **execution**. The platform may be accountable to the customer for resolving a failed dinner order while the restaurant is accountable for accurate availability and the provider is authoritative over an actual refund outcome. Accountability can remain even when an action is delegated to another system. Delegation does not mean transfer of business ownership.

A useful cross-check is **RACI**, but do not treat its cells as runtime permissions. “Accountable” identifies who answers for the result; “Responsible” identifies who performs the work; “Consulted” and “Informed” identify collaboration. An authorization policy answers a narrower question: which authenticated principal may trigger a specific action under specific conditions? RACI can inform authorization, but cannot replace its conditions.

### Capability does not prescribe a microservice

An enterprise can initially host ordering, restaurant decision capture and payment simulation inside one Java deployment with clear modules. It can also move one capability to a separate provider later. The capability map stays conceptually stable even when topology changes. Conversely, deploying three services cannot repair ambiguous refund ownership. You would simply distribute ambiguity across APIs.

Before implementation, produce an **ownership sentence**: “Under policy P, actor A may request operation X; business authority B decides eligibility; source C confirms externally controlled outcome; the platform records and communicates it.” The sentence is more valuable than naming a Queue or Controller.

## Understand — Decision rights, source of truth, and observable claims

There are at least four layers of authority in a transaction. **Request authority** asks who may initiate an operation. **Policy authority** asks who sets the rule governing that operation. **Decision authority** asks who accepts or rejects the specific request. **Fact authority** asks whose evidence establishes that an outcome actually occurred. These layers often belong to different actors.

Take a cancellation before restaurant acceptance. The customer may have permission to **request** it. The platform applies a documented merchant/platform policy to determine eligibility at that stage. The restaurant controls whether it accepted fulfillment. If there is already an externally confirmed charge, the provider controls evidence of subsequent refund completion. The platform may display the combined state; the display is a read model of multiple facts, not an independent financial authority.

A **source of truth** is scoped to a fact. Restaurant inventory and capacity facts may be authoritative at the merchant; the platform maintains an observed menu snapshot with freshness limits. Payment authorization, capture, settlement and refund should not be collapsed into a generic “PAID” flag. Even when a provider returns success, a later disputed settlement may require reconciliation. The exact semantics must come from the provider contract.

**Authority is not the same as holding a copy.** A support agent sees a payment history and can initiate a refund workflow, yet cannot edit the external payment ledger to make it true. A database can hold stale merchant availability. A delivery app can report a handoff event while the customer disputes receipt. We must retain the provenance, timestamps, and uncertainty of these observations as we design real systems later.

Beware the phrase “single source of truth for an order.” It may be sensible for the Order's own lifecycle facts but not for every related financial, merchant and delivery fact. An order screen is usually a composed view. A Staff-level reviewer should ask: **truth about which proposition, at what moment, and under whose authority?**

### Business policy is not yet a security mechanism

Writing “only the restaurant may accept” is a business rule. A future application must authenticate which principal represents that restaurant, authorize that principal for the target order, prevent stale decisions, and generate auditable evidence. Lesson 1.1.1 defines the policy and ownership; future lessons implement guards and test their concurrency properties. We do not claim authentication, authorization or transaction guarantees from today's diagram.

## Explore — Build and challenge the authority map

In the interactive **Actor & Capability Atlas** below, choose a participating actor and inspect what it wants, must do, may decide, and merely observes. Then select a business decision and reassign the proposed authority.

The atlas compares your proposal with an **explicit illustrative baseline**, not with an eternal product rule. A warning means “this proposal conflicts with the currently documented training policy or source-of-fact assignment.” It does not imply that a stakeholder could never define an alternate policy.

Watch the distinction between **requesting cancellation** (customer action), **deciding fulfillment** (restaurant authority), **initiating financial remediation** (platform/support under policy), and **confirming a provider refund** (external provider fact). A wrong assignment can create a dangerous system even if its UI makes perfect sense. For example, giving the platform sole authority to declare settlement is not repaired by adding more logs.

**Worked mapping:** Restaurant acceptance: customer expresses purchase intent; restaurant operator is responsible for capacity-based fulfillment decision; platform conveys and records the decision; kitchen observes accepted work; customer observes result. Payment confirmation: platform can request/record, provider is authoritative for provider result, customer observes. These two facts may be represented on one order page without sharing authority.

If two actors appear to “own cancellation,” ask whether they own **different sub-decisions**. Customer may request; platform may apply published policy; merchant may approve exception. Do not erase the conflict by randomly selecting one database owner.

## Explore — Follow a complete order decision journey

The branching **Workflow Studio** is an explicitly simplified teaching model, not our actual Java application. For the exercise, we assume: the platform receives the order request; restaurant makes the fulfillment decision; **payment is requested after restaurant acceptance**; kitchen starts only after payment confirmation; delivery begins after preparation. These are chosen scenario assumptions, not universal ordering requirements.

Choose whether the restaurant accepts or rejects. Then choose whether the provider authorizes, declines, or leaves the outcome **unknown**. Finally, explore cancellation after acceptance. The timeline must not equate “unknown” with “failed,” “rejected order” with “refunded payment,” or “refund requested” with “refund confirmed.” This lets you see exactly where a missing authority or policy decision changes the business promise.

**Path A — Restaurant rejects:** no payment attempt should be generated under this particular policy; customer should receive a rejection reason. If a charge is nevertheless observed, that is an incident requiring investigation rather than a normal success path.

**Path B — Restaurant accepts, provider declines:** merchant acceptance does not establish successful payment; preparation should not proceed under this example policy. The platform must communicate a failure or next step without inventing a provider-approved payment.

**Path C — Provider returns unknown:** the platform cannot safely infer a charge was declined or succeeded. It must retain a pending/unresolved condition and seek evidence through a provider-supported reconciliation mechanism. We do not implement that mechanism here.

**Path D — Late cancellation after payment:** a merchant-approved exception may create a refund **obligation**, not a confirmed refund. If the provider's outcome is pending, the customer-facing statement should be “refund requested/pending” rather than “money returned.” A strict cancellation policy instead rejects the late request, potentially raising a dispute. These are legitimate business alternatives with different costs and incentives.

In each path, identify who initiates, who decides, who attests to the fact, and who owes the user an explanation. The story is a collaboration of independently owned responsibilities, not a single giant enum.

## Predict — Give ownership to the right party

In the **Authority Challenge**, choose the party that should control a particular business decision or fact **under the stated policy**. Submit your answer before revealing the rationale. Correct answers alone are not enough: read why the tempting alternative is wrong, and identify cases where the answer depends on a negotiated merchant/platform policy.

You should confidently distinguish: the customer placing a request; the restaurant accepting or rejecting fulfillment; the external provider confirming a payment or refund operation; the platform applying a published cancellation rule; and a potentially unresolved post-acceptance exception policy.

Every case has a *stated decision verb*. If the case changes from **initiate a refund** to **confirm a refund succeeded**, the authority changes even though both may be called “refund” in a poorly designed API. This is the kind of modeling ambiguity that later becomes a production incident.

### Before revealing answers, write an ownership sentence

“Actor A may initiate X. Actor B decides Y under policy P. Actor C supplies authoritative evidence of Z. D is responsible for explaining the result to the customer.” If you cannot specify all four, document a policy gap rather than writing a controller.

## Diagnose — Two parties claim control of cancellation

**Incident briefing (fictional):** A customer receives “Cancellation successful.” The restaurant's tablet shows “Accepted, begin preparation,” and later issues a chargeback complaint. Someone proposes “add a retry queue to keep both applications synchronized.” That proposal skips the fundamental question: **Were the parties authorized to make contradictory promises under the same policy?**

Construct at least three competing hypotheses before diagnosing. (1) Business policy is ambiguous: customer and merchant screens implement different cancellation windows. (2) A correct policy exists but an application guard or timing rule is missing. (3) The messages are consistent with distinct legitimate events, but the UI presents a stale or mislabelled view. These hypotheses require different evidence. Network timing alone cannot prove that business authority was allocated correctly.

A strong first investigation collects the published cancellation policy and effective version, the customer's requested action and acknowledgement, merchant acceptance decision and its authority, state-transition ordering, and provider financial evidence **separately**. Exact logs and timestamps become essential once the application exists, but an engineer should know *which questions* those logs must answer.

In the interactive **Incident Caseboard**, choose which evidence to inspect and compare how it changes the plausibility of a hypothesis. Do not conclude “the database is inconsistent” simply because two screens differ; determine whether their displayed claims refer to the same business proposition and whether a policy contradiction existed.

### Diagnostic trap

A network timeout after a payment request is a technical observation. “The card was not charged” is a business assertion that may be false. Calling every timeout “payment failed” can induce retries and double effects. The authority model says whose evidence you need; later lessons teach how to obtain it safely.

### Expected diagnosis output

Produce a short causal account: symptom → affected participants → competing hypotheses → minimum discriminating evidence → provisional cause → policy/guard correction or operational mitigation → what remains unknown. Avoid retrofitting one observed symptom into a universal architectural failure story.

## Engineer — Domain discovery artifacts before Java

This is deliberately a **Type C** checkpoint. We do not initialize a Spring project here. The meaningful engineering output is a reviewed business domain contract that will constrain the first runnable slice in Lesson 1.1.2.

The companion domain artifacts describe: actors and incentives; capability ownership; an actor/decision/fact authority matrix; representative success, rejection and uncertain-outcome scenarios; known authority conflicts; and a policy question register. They are stored in the academy and will be linked to an immutable Java application checkpoint once the platform begins evolving.

**Scope-control example:** “Restaurant may reject unavailable items” can be a discovery requirement. “Implement a separate restaurant microservice with Kafka to handle rejections” is a design choice without a demonstrated pressure. We will initially keep these responsibilities in one deployable with separate conceptual modules where relevant; whether that topology changes depends on future evidence.

At the boundary to Lesson 1.1.2, the team must at least agree on a first vertical slice: who can request an order, who can determine restaurant acceptance, what completion means, whether payment occurs before or after acceptance in the first demo, and which outcomes are explicitly simulated. Not every business policy needs to be resolved now, but unresolved policies must not be silently coded as universal rules.

This distinction is fundamental: **a software project can evolve meaningfully through verified domain understanding even when no implementation has changed**. The next lesson earns its code change by converting these explicit outcomes into a runnable, tested command/use-case slice.

## Verify — Evidence you can and cannot claim

Because no Java domain behavior is executed for Lesson 1.1.1, the evidence is **structural and analytical**. We can verify that our role/capability model covers known parties, that decisions have proposed owners, and that deliberately ambiguous policies are recorded. We can test the interactive model’s deterministic functions and browser controls. None of these proves that the Food Ordering Platform enforces authorization or payment correctness at runtime.

The authority table is a hypothesis that requires stakeholder review. A **consistent table** is not necessarily a *correct policy*. Validate it through merchant interviews, finance/provider contract review, customer promise wording and controlled example walkthroughs.

There are three separate verification questions: (1) Is the **domain specification** coherent and explicit? (2) Does the **learning app** truthfully implement its documented toy model, with tests showing outcomes change when learner controls change? (3) Does a **real backend** enforce the corresponding policy under concurrency and failure? In this lesson, we can address the first two; the third is intentionally deferred.

### Peer-review checklist

Can each actor describe its outcome, decision rights and dependency? Does every external fact retain its authoritative source? Are request/approval/confirmation separated? Are unresolved cancellation and refund policies explicit? Do example scenarios detect contradictory promises? Can a newcomer say *what is unknown* without inventing a microservice or a guarantee?

A green React build proves only that code compiles and bundles. A browser test proves a particular route and control worked under its checked conditions. Neither substitutes for a business-policy approval or a real financial transaction.

## Defend — Communicate at multiple engineering levels

**60-second explanation for a colleague:** “I model the business actors first because a workflow is a collaboration between people and organizations with different goals. The restaurant decides whether it can fulfill an order; the customer requests a purchase and may request cancellation; the payment provider confirms its own financial operations; and platform operations must explain and remediate the composite customer experience. I separate who requests, who decides, and who confirms a fact. Then I write those responsibilities as a policy and scenario map before choosing API or service boundaries.”

**Senior backend discussion:** Be ready to explain how a restaurant owner, restaurant operator, kitchen worker, and restaurant data record differ; how authorization scopes relate to roles; and why a complete HTTP request does not prove provider completion. You should be able to derive candidate module interfaces without pretending they are deployment boundaries.

**Staff/Principal design challenge:** “Why not let Payment Service own the payment status?” Answer with scoped facts. The platform's payment module may *record* payment attempts and normalized provider responses, but the provider is authoritative for external financial results. Ask whether the question concerns authorization, capture, settlement, refund, or an internal risk decision. Model disagreement and reconciliation, and specify which guarantees the provider contract actually supports. Reject generic “source of truth” claims that omit the proposition.

**Executive/stakeholder explanation:** Focus on operational accountability: what the customer can reliably expect, when the restaurant may reject, who carries cancellation cost, what “refund pending” means, and what escalation path exists. Avoid discussing aggregates and Kafka unless they have consequences the audience can act on.

### An adversarial follow-up

“The restaurant reports a successful refund, but the customer bank statement shows a charge. Who is wrong?” Neither is conclusively wrong without knowing which operation was initiated, what the provider has confirmed, the bank posting delay, and the source and time of both displays. Your next action is to specify the discriminating evidence and customer communication, not to overwrite a status field.

## Retain — Transfer, retrieval, and mastery gate

After this lesson, close the content and reconstruct a **role/capability/authority table** from memory for a restaurant ordering scenario. Include at least seven actors or roles and five distinct business decisions. Do not reuse “owns payment” without stating whether you mean initiating, authorizing, capturing, recording or confirming a refund.

Next, transfer the same reasoning to a **hotel reservation**. Guest requests a room; property inventory determines whether it can be sold; the booking platform composes the experience; a payment provider confirms its own operations; property staff perform check-in; support resolves exceptions. Ask which participant can cancel after check-in, who can reverse an already completed charge, and whether “reservation confirmed” is the same as “room access granted.” The actor names change, but authority reasoning survives.

Finally, test the model adversarially: what if the restaurant accepts after the customer cancellation notice; what if a support agent initiates a refund without merchant approval; what if a provider response is delayed; what if delivery disputes receipt? State the policy assumption, authoritative fact, evidence needed, and why a software module cannot simply invent an answer.

**Mastery is demonstrated when you can:** distinguish actor/stakeholder/role/entity/component; explain incentives and conflicts; draw a complete actor-to-capability map; name requester, decision authority and fact source; diagnose a conflicting-cancellation promise; defend why capability boundaries are not microservice boundaries; and communicate the same architecture to a technical reviewer and a business stakeholder.

The output of this lesson is **a documented domain contract, not a runnable backend**. Continue to Lesson 1.1.2 only after you can articulate the first credible order slice and its explicit assumptions.
